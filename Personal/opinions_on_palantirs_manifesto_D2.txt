Do I agree with John Locke's declaration of which "the highest perfection of intellectual natures lies in a careful and constant pursuit of true and solid happiness"? Before I disagree, its best to break down what that might look like for any given individual. All humans are inherently intellectual beings, as Jefferson wrote "all men are created equal", and as such every human has the ability to pursue their own perfect world, whatever that may appear as for themselves. Even for indivduals riddled with incompetency of which has illuded them from a true pursuit of happiness will argue that they strive to do what makes them happy. This would mean that every humans version of "the highest perfect of intellectual nature" is whatever they deem to be the path towards their own happiness. If that is raising a family, an individuals "highest perfection" is succeeding in having a family. If what someone wants is to raise a family of which is socially acceptable, then they will find their highest perfection of intellectual nature by being a true provider and raising their own to be the next generation of socially acceptable individuals. As such, it is relatively unjust for me to look down on any others version of "highest perfection of intellectual nature", as we are all born as equal individuals with our own morals, experiences, and dreams. That being said, I believe John Locke misused "intellectual nature" in this context. It does not take an intellectual nature to raise a socially acceptable family, in the same way it does not take an intellectual nature to pursue drugs as your "true and solid happiness". 
As such, I believe John Locke is incorrect in his statement. Any true intellectual knows three things: 
(a) an intellectual strives for the advencement of their own understanding of the world, and is under constant realization of how insignificant their own knowledge is in comparison to the true capacity of human understanding. Even human understanding of the world has its limits, so a true intellectual must look to pioneer past our current understanding and shape a new perspective on the world, while simulataneously showing interest in learning what we already know. 
(b) an intellectual looks to hone his logical process. The hallmark of an intellectual is the ability to use their understanding of the world, extract context from any given situation, and apply it with a logical thought process to produce a new concept. This is just to say, a real intellectual is strong in their logical process, and is able to find patterns in the world of which can be used to come to a new conclusion. 
(c) a true intellectual strives to be correct. I believe the passion of correctness is lost in our society, perhaps more absent than the two previous prerequisites. A true intellectual is not only proud of their logical prowess, but must strive to have an understanding as many variables and knowledge of as much context of any given subject to make a logical assesment. The better your data is, the deeper your understanding is, the better you can make a logical connection among your context, allows for a higher possibility in your logical deduction to be correct. A true intellectual enjoys the process of learning, thinking, and being correct about their thinking. 

As such, I do not think that John Locke could use the phrase "highest perfection of intellectual nature" in this context. It is an unfortunate truth in this world that most of the population will never have access to the breadth of knowledge that humans have created over our conscious lifespan. I completely agree that all humans are created equal and independent, and I believe that every human has the capability to maximize their own intellectual prowess, however it is unfortunate that the art of intellectualism (predominantly in western society) is so far lost from what it originally was. Just as Feynman argued, "Never confuse education with intelligence. You can have a Ph.D. and still be an idiot". The art of education in true intellectualism has largely been lost in western society, and I believe that Locke using intellectual nature as a definition an individuals personal goals is incorrect. Of course, you can find happiness in your life without being an "intellectual", often enough in our world the smartest people are not those with a degree.
